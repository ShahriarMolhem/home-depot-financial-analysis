# Home Depot — Slide-by-Slide Speaking Script

**Presenter:** Shahriar Molhem, BBA Finance Student, University of Georgia  
**Deck:** `home_depot_financial_analysis_presentation.pptx`  
**Purpose:** Practice a natural, accurate 8–10-minute undergraduate portfolio presentation.

> **Speaking instructions:** Read the paragraphs aloud. Do not read the slide's small source citations aloud. Pause briefly when changing slides. All valuation figures are *illustrative model outputs*, not current stock-price targets.

## Slide 1 — Financial Performance & Valuation (about 45 seconds)

Hi, and thanks for taking a look at my project. I'm Shahriar Molhem, a finance student at the University of Georgia. For this project, I examined Home Depot's financial performance from fiscal year 2021 through fiscal year 2025 using publicly available SEC filings.

I focused on three things: how the company's financial results changed, how effectively those results translated into cash flow, and how a basic valuation model responds to different assumptions.

This is an undergraduate finance case study, not a professional audit or an investment recommendation. My goal is to show how financial statements, Excel modeling, and clear reasoning can work together.

**Cue:** Smile, make eye contact, and introduce the three-part structure without rushing.

## Slide 2 — Research Design (about 50 seconds)

I organized this analysis around three questions.

First, what changed in Home Depot's financial performance? For that, I looked at revenue, operating income, and profit margins over five fiscal years.

Second, did cash flow tell the same story as reported earnings? I examined operating cash flow, capital expenditures, and some working-capital indicators.

Third, what might the business be worth under a clearly defined set of assumptions? I used a simplified discounted cash flow, or DCF, model and tested how sensitive the result was to different inputs.

Throughout the project, I tried to separate reported facts from calculated ratios, future assumptions, and my own interpretation.

**Cue:** Point to each of the three research questions as you discuss it.

## Slide 3 — Sales and Operating Income (about 55 seconds)

This slide shows one of the most important findings.

Home Depot's reported sales increased from about 151.2 billion dollars in fiscal 2021 to 164.7 billion dollars in fiscal 2025. That's a cumulative increase of approximately 8.9 percent.

However, operating income went in the opposite direction. It decreased from about 23.0 billion dollars to 20.9 billion dollars, or roughly 9.3 percent.

So, although the company generated more revenue by the end of the period, it did not generate more operating profit.

One important qualification is that fiscal 2024 had 53 weeks and the company completed acquisitions during the period. That means reported growth should not automatically be treated as organic growth.

**Cue:** Emphasize the contrast: **sales up, operating income down**.

## Slide 4 — Profitability (about 55 seconds)

To understand that difference better, I compared profit margins.

Operating margin is operating income divided by net sales. It declined from approximately 15.2 percent in fiscal 2021 to 12.7 percent in fiscal 2025, a reduction of about 2.6 percentage points.

Net margin also declined, from around 10.9 percent to 8.6 percent.

This suggests that higher reported sales did not translate into stronger profitability per dollar of revenue.

Selling, general, and administrative expenses became larger relative to sales, but I would not say that one factor alone explains the entire decline. For a fair interpretation, I also need to consider changes in business mix, acquisitions, and management's disclosures.

**Cue:** Say *percentage points*, not *percent*, when describing the margin decline.

## Slide 5 — Cash Generation (about 55 seconds)

Accounting profit is important, but I also wanted to understand the company's cash generation.

For fiscal 2025, Home Depot reported about 16.3 billion dollars of operating cash flow and approximately 3.7 billion dollars of capital expenditures.

If I subtract capital expenditures from operating cash flow, I get about 12.6 billion dollars of simplified free cash flow.

This measure helps us think about how much cash remains after investment in property and equipment. But it can fluctuate because of working capital and the timing of cash payments.

It's also important to distinguish this simple free cash flow measure from **unlevered free cash flow to the firm**, or FCFF, which I use later in the DCF model.

**Cue:** Pause after saying **$12.6 billion**. Explain the two definitions of cash flow clearly.

## Slide 6 — Forecast and DCF Method (about 75 seconds)

After reviewing historical performance, I built a simplified five-year cash flow forecast covering fiscal 2026 through 2030.

I started with actual fiscal 2025 sales of about 164.7 billion dollars and operating income of about 20.9 billion dollars. Those are historical reported figures.

Everything after that involves assumptions. My projected sales growth ranges from 2.8 to 3.2 percent per year, and my assumed operating margin gradually increases from 12.9 to 13.4 percent.

To calculate free cash flow to the firm, I start with operating profit after estimated taxes, which is called NOPAT. Then I add depreciation and amortization, subtract capital expenditures, and subtract increases in operating working capital.

Finally, I discount those future cash flows using an assumed 8 percent WACC, with a 2.5 percent perpetual growth rate.

Neither WACC nor the growth assumptions are company guidance. They are transparent inputs for this educational scenario.

**Cue:** Slow down on **NOPAT + D&A − Capex − change in NWC** and point to the formula on the slide.

## Slide 7 — Base-Case Valuation (about 65 seconds)

Here is the result of the base-case valuation.

The present value of the five explicitly forecast years of free cash flow is approximately 71.6 billion dollars. The discounted terminal value adds another 247.3 billion dollars.

Together, these produce an illustrative enterprise value of about 318.9 billion dollars.

Next, I subtract approximately 54.4 billion dollars of net financial debt, resulting in estimated equity value of about 264.5 billion dollars.

Dividing that amount by 996 million year-end shares gives a model value of **265 dollars and 54 cents per share**.

However, the most important takeaway is that about 77.6 percent of enterprise value comes from the terminal value. This makes the estimate highly dependent on what I assume happens after the explicit forecast period.

**Cue:** Present **$265.54** as a *model result*, never as today's share price or a recommendation.

## Slide 8 — Sensitivity Analysis (about 60 seconds)

Because the DCF depends so much on assumptions, I ran a sensitivity analysis.

The table shows how the implied value per share changes when I adjust WACC, which is the discount rate, and the perpetual growth rate.

The center cell represents my base case: 8 percent WACC, 2.5 percent perpetual growth, and a modeled value of 265 dollars and 54 cents.

Keeping WACC at 8 percent, reducing perpetual growth to 1.5 percent lowers the result to about 225 dollars. Increasing perpetual growth to 3.5 percent raises the result to approximately 324 dollars.

So the main lesson is not that one number is definitely correct. It's that valuation requires judgment and that even modest changes in assumptions can have a major effect.

**Cue:** Point to the middle row, then move left and right to show changing growth assumptions.

## Slide 9 — Critical Evaluation (about 65 seconds)

Before drawing conclusions, I want to highlight four limitations.

First, Home Depot had 53 weeks in fiscal 2024, which complicates year-over-year revenue comparisons.

Second, acquisitions such as SRS Distribution and GMS affect reported consolidated results. That means I cannot assume all reported growth came from the existing business.

Third, terminal value represents approximately 77.6 percent of enterprise value in my DCF. This creates substantial sensitivity to long-term assumptions.

And fourth, I used a simplified enterprise-to-equity bridge. I did not fully model operating leases, every possible balance-sheet adjustment, or a market-derived cost of capital.

These limitations are part of the analysis, not something to hide. A useful financial model should explain both what it tells us and what it cannot tell us.

**Cue:** Treat the limitations as evidence of good analytical judgment, not as an apology.

## Slide 10 — Conclusion (about 45 seconds)

To conclude, this project helped me connect three core finance skills.

First, I used primary SEC filings to understand historical business performance and financial statements.

Second, I organized the information into an Excel model that separates historical data, formulas, and forecast assumptions.

Third, I communicated the results using clear charts, a DCF valuation, and sensitivity analysis, while explaining the limitations of the model.

My biggest takeaway is that rising revenue doesn't automatically mean rising profitability, and a valuation is only as useful as the assumptions behind it.

Thank you for listening. I'm happy to walk through the workbook, discuss the assumptions, or explain what I would improve in a future version.

**Cue:** Finish calmly, make eye contact, and leave the final slide displayed for questions.

## Three-round practice routine

1. **Round 1 — Accuracy:** Read directly from this document, following every slide. Focus on saying the numbers and distinctions correctly.
2. **Round 2 — Confidence:** Practice with only slide headings and one cue per slide. Record yourself and target 8–10 minutes.
3. **Round 3 — Interview:** Present without reading, then explain three questions: *Why did margins fall? How does FCFF differ from operating cash flow minus capex? Why is terminal value so important?*

**Integrity note:** These are practice words, not evidence of independent expertise. Before claiming personal modeling experience, make sure you can reproduce and explain the calculations and identify where AI-assisted work was used.

## Delivery notes: make this sound like you

The full script above is a reference, not something to memorize word for word. The strongest version of this presentation sounds like a student walking someone through decisions and trade-offs.

- **After slide 3:** “So my next question was whether the higher sales were actually translating into better margins.”
- **After slide 5:** “That cash flow comparison is what led me to build a forward-looking scenario, rather than relying on earnings alone.”
- **Before slide 7:** “The model gives me a number, but the sensitivity is just as important as the number itself.”
- **Before slide 9:** “This is where I'd be careful if I were presenting the model in an investment discussion.”

**Three follow-up questions to rehearse:** Why does FY2024 having 53 weeks matter? What is the difference between OCF minus capex and unlevered FCFF? What happens if you raise WACC by one percentage point?

**How to answer when you don't know:** “I haven't modeled that adjustment yet. I'd check the corresponding 10-K note and update the assumptions before drawing a conclusion.”
